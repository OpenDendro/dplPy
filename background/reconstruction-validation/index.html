    Reconstruction validation¶

    A climate reconstruction is a model — tree-ring predictors mapped onto a climate target over a period where both are known, then extrapolated to centuries where only the tree rings exist. The question that matters is not whether the model fits the years it was built on, but whether it has genuine skill on years it has never seen. This page explains how pcreg answers that question, what each statistic means, and where dplPy follows or departs from the method in Ed Cook's OSR program (Cook, Briffa & Jones 1994).

    A note on the word¶

    dplPy says validation, not "verification". The distinction follows Oreskes, Shrader-Frechette & Belitz (1994): to verify (Latin verus, true) is to establish truth, which is possible only in a closed system. A reconstruction is a model of an open natural system, so its truth can never be established — only its legitimacy. "Validation" denotes exactly that, and it is the honest word for what these statistics do. dplPy's API reflects this: the withheld window is the validation= argument, and the result carries .validation. (Cook's Fortran subroutine is named verify; references to that routine keep its original name.)

    Split-sample validation¶

    The design is simple and strict. The overlap between the predictors and the climate target is divided into a calibration period, where the model is built (screen → PCA → regression → back-transform), and a validation period of withheld years the model never saw during fitting. Skill is judged only on the withheld years.

    In dplPy the two windows are stated explicitly:

    import dplpy as dpl
res = dpl.pcreg(predictors, target,
                calibration=(1965, 1989),
                validation=(1959, 1964))

    Cook's Fortran derives the validation period automatically as every overlap year outside the calibration window (a block before and a block after). dplPy deliberately requires the analyst to state it instead. The reason is data quality: an instrumental target may be unreliable in part of its record — a gridded PDSI that relaxes toward climatology before the dense-station era, say — and blindly validating against those years would inflate or wreck the skill for reasons that have nothing to do with the reconstruction. An explicit window lets a knowledgeable analyst fence off bad data; it is a control worth keeping.

    Calibration-period skill is not validation¶

    The calibration R² (result.cal_r2) — the fraction of target variance the model explains in sample — is reported, but it is a floor, not a verdict. A model with many predictors screened over few years can fit the calibration period well and still have no out-of-sample skill. The validation statistics exist precisely because calibration fit can mislead.

    The validation statistics¶

    All of the following are computed on the withheld years, in result.stats, for both the raw series and (see below) its first differences.

    Reduction of Error (RE) is the central statistic. It compares the reconstruction's error against the error of simply guessing the calibration mean every year:

    RE = 1 − Σ(observed − reconstructed)² / Σ(observed − calibration_mean)²

    RE runs from −∞ to 1. Anything above 0 means the reconstruction beats climatology on the withheld years. It is a sensitive test — a low bar, and the usual minimum threshold for claiming any skill.

    Coefficient of Efficiency (CE) is the same ratio with a stricter baseline: the validation-period mean rather than the calibration mean.

    CE = 1 − Σ(observed − reconstructed)² / Σ(observed − validation_mean)²

    Because the validation-period mean is the best constant predictor for those years, CE is harder to pass, and \(\mathrm{CE} \le \mathrm{RE}\) in general. The gap between them reflects how much the calibration and validation means differ; a positive CE is the stronger claim of skill.

    Correlations. Pearson r measures linear agreement; Spearman and Kendall are rank-based and insensitive to a few outliers; the robust correlation is the biweight midcorrelation, which down-weights outliers directly. Each is reported with a one-sided significance (_p) — a valid reconstruction is expected to correlate positively, so the test is one-tailed.

    Sign test counts how many withheld years place the actual and the reconstruction on the same side of the mean, against a 50/50 coin — pure directional agreement. The product-means test (Fritts) is a weighted version, asking whether the same-sign agreements dominate. The equality-of-means test is a Welch t-test that the reconstruction is not biased — that its mean over the withheld years matches the actual mean.

    High-frequency skill: the first-difference pass¶

    Every statistic is computed a second time on the year-to-year differences of both series (the _diff keys in result.stats). This is the stricter view. A reconstruction can look excellent on levels purely by sharing a low-frequency trend with the target, while having no real interannual skill. Differencing removes the trend and asks whether the reconstruction tracks the wiggles. Strong level agreement with weak differenced agreement is a warning that the skill is all in the trend.

    Cross-validation¶

    Split-sample validation uses one withheld window. Cross-validation (cv=) instead rotates the held-out years through the calibration period: with cv=1 it leaves out one year at a time (leave-one-out), with cv=k it leaves out contiguous k-year blocks, and — crucially — it refits the entire pipeline (screening, PCA, regression) on the remaining years each time before predicting the held-out ones. The pooled held-out predictions give cv_r, cv_RE and cv_CE. Because nothing from a held-out year informs its own prediction, cross-validation exposes overfitting that the in-sample R² hides: a model that screens hundreds of predictors over a few decades can show a high calibration R² and a negative cv_RE.

    What dplPy implements, and where it departs from Cook's OSR¶

    dplPy reproduces Cook's validation design — split-sample, the full statistical panel, RE against the calibration mean and CE against the validation mean, and the first-difference pass — and it was checked number-for-number against the compiled OSR Fortran on a single-target fixture. It then makes a few deliberate, documented departures, all in the direction of correctness over exact reproduction (see Fidelity & departures for the package-wide principle):

    Area dplPy Cook's OSR Why
    Significance SciPy's exact distributions Wilson-Hilferty / normal approximations The approximations are era-driven and visibly off at low degrees of freedom; a close-call predictor can land the other side of a screening threshold, but exact reproduction of a 1990s approximation is not the goal.
    Robust correlation Biweight midcorrelation (Wilcox; = astropy's) Ed's sum/difference biweight construction The biweight midcorrelation is the documented, standard robust correlation.
    Validation window Explicit validation= Auto-derived from the calibration/data overlap Analyst control over data quality (above).
    Cross-validation Leave-k-out refitting the whole pipeline A 301-sample bootstrap of the final pairs A refit CV is the rigorous out-of-sample test; the bootstrap resamples the fitted pairs only.
    Precision float64 throughout real*4 dplPy never down-casts to reproduce single-precision rounding.

    Two features of the Fortran are intentionally not ported: the automatic two-block (pre- and post-calibration) validation split — a single explicit window plus cross-validation covers the practical need — and canonical regression, which is deferred.
