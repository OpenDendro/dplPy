    Moving correlation¶

    A climate signal in a tree-ring chronology is often assumed to be constant: we calibrate over the instrumental period and reconstruct the past on the strength of that one number. But the correlation between growth and climate can change — a site can lose (or gain) sensitivity to a season as the climate, the stand, or the trees themselves change. A moving correlation looks for that: it slides a window along the two series and correlates them inside each window, producing a trace of the relationship through time.

    The trace is easy to compute and easy to over-read. dpl.moving_corr is built around the second problem as much as the first. A moving correlation on overlapping windows wobbles even when the true relationship is perfectly constant, so the interesting-looking excursions in a raw trace are partly, and sometimes entirely, an artifact of the method. The function pairs the trace with significance tests designed for exactly this situation, and a figure that shows the uncertainty rather than hiding it.

    Three things to keep in mind¶

    Overlapping windows manufacture wobble (the Slutsky-Yule effect). Adjacent windows share all but one or two years, so successive points on the trace are highly dependent, and a short-window correlation of two persistent (red) series drifts up and down on its own. Averaging and differencing operations impose their own low-frequency rhythm on random data — Slutsky and Yule showed this a century ago — and a moving correlation is such an operation. The consequence: a smoothly undulating trace is the expected behaviour of the null, not evidence against it. The remedy here is to make the null wobble the same way. The significance tests compare the observed trace against surrogate series that carry the same persistence (lag-1 autocorrelation) of both your chronology and your climate variable, the same background correlation, and the same window and offset. Whatever wobble overlapping windows induce is present in the null too, so the test asks only whether the observed trace wobbles more than that.

    You are running many tests at once (multiplicity). A trace of 76 windows is 76 correlations; scanning it for the highest one and testing that is not the same as testing a single pre-chosen window, and the season grid (several season lengths × twelve ending months) multiplies the count again. Flagging each window that individually clears a 95% line guarantees false positives. moving_corr handles the within-trace multiplicity with a peak test whose null is the maximum correlation anywhere in each surrogate trace: a single threshold line then already accounts for having searched the whole record, so a point above it is significant at the stated level across the entire trace, not just at one spot.

    "Significant" and "not significant" is not a real boundary (Gelman & Stern). The difference between a window that clears the line and one that just misses it is not itself significant, and a map of significant/not-significant cells invites you to read a change in the signal where there may only be a change in the p-value. So moving_corr does not shade per-window significance. It shows the estimate with its uncertainty — the trace, a per-window confidence band, and one threshold line — and reports the whole-trace tests as single verdicts. The question it answers is "is this trace, as a whole, more structured than chance," not "which individual years are significant."

    How it works¶

    1. Seasonalize. The monthly climate variable is collapsed into one seasonal series using the same machinery as seascorr: you give an ending month and a season length (e.g. ending month 8, length 3 = Jun–Aug), and whether to aggregate by "mean" (temperature-like) or "sum" (precipitation totals).
    2. Slide and correlate. The chronology and the seasonal series are aligned on their common years, and a window of window years is stepped along them by offset years; each window yields one correlation. offset=1 gives a fully overlapping, smooth trace; offset=window gives adjacent, non-overlapping (independent) windows; values between trade smoothness for independence. The point is plotted at the window's centre by default (position).
    3. Build the matched null. For the significance tests, many surrogate pairs are generated at the observed background correlation with each series' lag-1 persistence (the red-noise null; noise="white" drops the persistence), and the same moving correlation — same window, same offset — is computed for each.
    4. Test the whole trace. Three global statistics of the observed trace are compared against their null distributions: its standard deviation (is the relationship more variable than chance — the Gershunov et al. 2001 test), its maximum (is the strongest window real), and its high-to-low range.

    A worked example¶

    Give moving_corr a year-indexed chronology and one monthly climate variable:

    import dplpy as dpl

res = dpl.moving_corr(crn, precip, end_month=8, season_length=3, agg="sum",
                      window=30, offset=1, noise="red", seed=1, plot=True)

res.trace        # DataFrame: window position year -> start, end, r, ci_lower/upper
res.tests        # the global tests: "std", "peak", "range"
res.overall_corr # full-period correlation
res.threshold(0.95)        # the upper (peak) significance line
res.threshold_lower(0.95)  # the lower (trough) significance line
print(res.summary())

    The figure (also drawn by res.plot()) shows the correlation trace in blue with its per-window confidence band, the overall correlation, and the peak/trough threshold lines; windows that cross a threshold are dotted. The whole-trace test verdicts are printed in the caption — as statements about the trace, not about individual windows.

    Choices you make¶

    Window and offset. window sets the width in years. A longer window (30 is the default, a climate-normal length) gives a smoother, better-estimated trace but resolves change more slowly and needs a longer record; a shorter window is more responsive but noisier, with wide per-window intervals. offset is the overlap control: keep it at 1 for a continuous trace, or raise it toward window when you want windows that are closer to independent. The null is always computed at the same window and offset, so the test stays fair whichever you choose.

    The null model. noise="red" (the default) matches both series' lag-1 persistence and is the right null for persistent climate and tree-ring series; noise="white" is the classical Gershunov null and will flag more traces as "unstable" precisely because it ignores the wobble persistence induces.

    The peak test's question. peak_condition="observed" (the default) asks whether the strongest window is elevated above the established background relationship; peak_condition="zero" asks whether any relationship exists at all. Once you already know two series are related overall, the zero-conditioned test is easy to pass and says little about the peak being special.

    The confidence band. ci="fisher" (the default) is an analytic Fisher-z interval at an effective sample size reduced for autocorrelation, so short windows honestly show wide bands; ci="bootstrap" is a moving-block bootstrap; ci="none" omits the band.

    Reproducibility. The surrogates and the bootstrap are stochastic; pass a seed= for reproducible significance. The trace itself is deterministic.

    Reading the output¶

    A wobbling trace is not, by itself, evidence that the climate signal is unstable — that is the whole reason the tests exist. Read the trace together with the verdicts: if the std test is not significant, the variation you see is within what red noise and overlapping windows produce, however structured it looks. A single window crossing a line is weak; a sustained, physically-sensible shift (for instance a signal that strengthens after a known stand disturbance, or weakens in a late-century divergence) supported by a significant whole-trace test is the strong case. The per-window band is a reminder that each point is itself uncertain, especially for short windows.

    The season grid¶

    Everything above is one chronology against one season. To ask the stability question across the whole seasonal grid — every season length by every ending month, as in seascorr — pass season_lengths (plural) instead of a single season_length:

    grid = dpl.moving_corr(crn, precip, end_month=12, season_lengths=(1, 3, 6, 12),
                       window=30, agg="sum", seed=1)

grid.grid        # one row per season: overall_r, stability_p, fdr_sig, ...
grid.cube        # the moving-correlation trace of every season
grid.field       # field significance (see below)
grid.plot(kind="small_multiples", seasons=[(3, 5), (3, 8)])
grid.plot(kind="dominant")
grid.plot(kind="surface")
print(grid.summary())

    This returns a MovingCorrGridResult with three complementary views, none of which uses a per-cell significance mask:

    • small_multiples (the default) tiles the single-season trace-with-band plot across the seasons you pass in seasons= (a list of (length, end_month)), each panel an honest estimate-and-uncertainty display with * marking the FDR-significant seasons. With no seasons= it warns and falls back to the strongest-correlating season at each length.
    • dominant shows, for one season length, which ending month carries the strongest correlation at each window — the trace of the "winning" season over time, coloured by its strength, with a grey bar spanning the seasons within 90 % of the best (narrow where the choice is sharp, wide where it is ambiguous). It answers "does the dominant season shift," but because it always draws a confident-looking line, read it together with the field-significance verdict.
    • surface is the full picture: a season-by-time heatmap of the moving correlation on a diverging red/blue scale (red positive, white near zero), so you see at a glance which seasons carry the signal and how it changes through time — never a grid of significance flags flickering from cell to cell. Pass mode="anomaly" to map each cell's departure from its own full-period correlation instead, which flattens the magnitude to emphasise change alone.

    Field significance¶

    Scanning dozens of seasons across many windows is a severe multiple-comparisons problem, made worse because neighbouring seasons share months and so move together. dplPy reports two things, for two different questions. The Livezey-Chen field test is the grid-level headline: it counts the locally-significant cells and compares that count against the distribution produced by a chronology unrelated to climate (reusing the real climate grid, so the inter-season correlation is preserved), answering "is this chronology's seasonal signal, as a whole, more non-stationary than noise?" The Benjamini-Hochberg FDR set is the per-season annotation — which individual seasons survive multiplicity — shown as the * marks and the red surface labels. The point, again in the spirit of Gelman & Stern (2006), is a grid-level verdict plus honest per-season effect sizes, not a thresholded map that invites you to read the on/off pattern as the signal.

    Fidelity¶

    The significance engine is a port of the moving_corr_sig package (K. Anchukaitis), validated against Table 1 of Gershunov et al. (2001): for two white-noise series correlated at 0.30 with an 11-year window, the 95th percentile of the null standard-deviation distribution is ≈ 0.36. dplPy's port reproduces that package bit-for-bit given the same random stream — the moving correlation, the surrogate generation, the AR(1) fit, and the Monte-Carlo ensemble all match — and the moving correlation itself matches a direct rolling correlation to machine precision.
