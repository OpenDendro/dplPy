    Climate–growth response¶

    dpl.seascorr identifies the seasonal climate signal in a tree-ring chronology: it correlates the chronology against a primary and a secondary monthly climate variable across many candidate seasons and flags which relationships are significant. It is a faithful port of SEASCORR (Meko, Touchan & Anchukaitis 2011, Computers & Geosciences 37, 1234–1241), the first step in moving from a dated chronology toward a climate reconstruction.

    The chronology is correlated (simple Pearson) with the primary variable and (partial, controlling for the primary) with the secondary variable, for every combination of a set of season-lengths and 14 ending months. Significance is assessed by exact simulation: the chronology is simulated many times in a way that preserves its spectrum, and each observed correlation is compared with the distribution of correlations from the surrogates.

    Basic usage¶

    import dplpy as dpl

crn = dpl.chron(dpl.detrend(dpl.readers("site.rwl")))   # a chronology
result = dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=precip, secondary=temperature)
result.table          # one row per season: correlations, p-values, significance

    crn is a year-indexed Series, or a DataFrame with column= naming the index series. primary and secondary are each one monthly climate variable. The default pairing follows the paper — precipitation primary (summed over a season), temperature secondary (averaged):

    result = dpl.seascorr(
    crn, primary=precip, secondary=temperature,
    end_month=9,                      # last month of the growth year
    season_lengths=(1, 3, 6, 12),     # months; the first must be 1
    primary_agg="sum", secondary_agg="mean",
    nsim=1000,                        # exact simulations; >= 1000
    seed=42,                          # reproducible significance
)

    To put temperature first (simple correlation on temperature, partial on precipitation), just swap the arguments — there is no role flag:

    result = dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=temperature, secondary=precip,
                      primary_agg="mean", secondary_agg="sum")

    Climate input formats¶

    Each climate variable may be supplied in whichever shape you already have; all three normalize internally to the same monthly series:

    # wide: a year index/column + 12 month columns (a station CSV)
dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=wide_df, secondary=temp_df)

# tidy long: columns year, month, value
dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=tidy_df, secondary=temp_df)

# datetime-indexed monthly series (e.g. a grid-point extraction)
pre = ds["pre"].sel(lat=36.1, lon=8.5, method="nearest").to_series()
dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=pre, secondary=tmp)

    Units are your responsibility (scale e.g. tenths of mm to mm before calling); primary_agg/secondary_agg then set whether a season is a sum or a mean.

    The result¶

    seascorr returns a SeascorrResult. Its main payload is result.table, a tidy DataFrame with one row per season:

    Column Meaning
    season_length, end_month, label the season (e.g. Mar–May, Oct* for a prior-year ending month)
    r_primary, p_primary simple correlation with the primary variable and its empirical non-exceedance probability
    r_partial, p_partial partial correlation with the secondary variable (controlling for the primary) and its probability
    r_clim Pearson r between the primary and secondary variable
    sig05_*, sig01_* two-sided significance flags at α = 0.05 / 0.01

    Also on the result: result.highest (the strongest primary-variable season per season-length), result.acf (lag 1–5 autocorrelation of the chronology), and result.period.

    The bar chart¶

    With plot=True (the default) seascorr draws the one figure the method is built around — the two-panel seasonal bar chart (simple correlations on top, partial on the bottom), significance color-coded. Bar color follows the variable: temperature is red, moisture (precipitation, PDSI, …) blue, whichever is primary. It is auto-detected from primary_name/secondary_name, and can be forced:

    res = dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=temperature, secondary=precip,
                   primary_name="Temperature", secondary_name="Precipitation")
res.fig.savefig("seascorr.png", dpi=300)       # fig and axes hang off the result

# force colors, or a black-and-white version for print
dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=p, secondary=t, primary_color="blue",
             secondary_color="red")
dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=p, secondary=t, plot=True, ... )   # color=False in the plot call for B/W

    Each panel's y-axis is fixed at ±0.3 and expands only to accommodate larger correlations, so a weak, non-significant partial panel still reads on a stable scale.

    Temporal stability¶

    Pass an early and a late window as period to test whether the strongest season's correlation is stable between the two sub-periods (Fisher-z difference of correlations, with effective sample sizes adjusted for autocorrelation after Dawdy & Matalas 1964):

    res = dpl.seascorr(crn, primary=precip, secondary=temp,
                   period=((1903, 1952), (1953, 2002)))
res.stability     # one row per season-length: r_full/early/late, Δz, p_value

    A single window, period=(first, last), just sets the analysis period; omitting period uses the full overlap of chronology and climate.

    Things to know¶

    • Per-season significance. Each of the seasons is tested independently at its own α; seascorr does not apply a multiple-comparison correction, faithful to Meko et al. (2011). Read a lone significant season among many with that in mind.
    • Exact simulation. It is the chronology that is simulated (preserving its spectrum), so the null is "a tree-ring series with this spectrum, unrelated to climate." nsim must be at least 1000 so the tail probabilities can resolve α = 0.01. Set seed= for reproducible p-values.
    • Partial correlations behave very slightly liberally over heavily overlapping seasons; treat marginal partial-correlation significance cautiously.
    • Data requirements. The chronology must be gap-free over the analysis window and at least 31 years long (fill interior gaps with fill_internal first). Each climate variable needs complete monthly coverage over the years it contributes; because long seasons reach into the prior year, the usable window starts about two years after the first year of complete climate data.
    • Non-Gaussian chronologies still run, with a warning — the exact-simulation method assumes an approximately Gaussian series.
