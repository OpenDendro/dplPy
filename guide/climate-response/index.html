    Correlation and response functions¶

    A correlation or response function is the classic monthly screen of a chronology against climate: one coefficient for each month of one or more climate variables, running from the previous growing season into the current one, each with a confidence interval or significance flag, read as a bar chart across the months. It answers "which months is this site's growth responding to, and how strongly." dpl.climate_response computes it, by either of the two standard methods, with bootstrapped significance.

    The idea goes back to Fritts et al. (1971) and the program DENDROCLIM2002 (Biondi & Waikul 2004), and the interface here follows the one many users know from the R package treeclim (Zang & Biondi 2015). dplPy's results match treeclim's to machine precision (see Validation below).

    Correlation function or response function¶

    Both relate the chronology to the individual months of the climate data; they differ in how they handle the fact that neighbouring months are themselves correlated.

    The correlation function (method="correlation") is the simplest thing: the Pearson correlation of the chronology with each month separately. It is easy to read, but because, say, a warm June and a warm July tend to go together, two adjacent months can both correlate with growth even if only one of them is doing the work — the correlation function cannot tell them apart.

    The response function (method="response", the default) is Fritts' answer to that. Instead of correlating month by month, it regresses the chronology on the whole monthly-climate matrix at once, so each month's coefficient is its contribution after accounting for the others. A plain multiple regression on collinear months would be unstable, so the predictors are first rotated to their principal components (which are uncorrelated by construction), the chronology is regressed on those, and the coefficients are transformed back to one weight per month. The number of components kept is set by the PVP criterion (Guiot 1991): keep the leading components while the running product of their eigenvalues stays above one. Dropping the trailing components is what regularizes the fit against the near-collinearity of adjacent months; keeping all of them would just return the unstable standardized multiple-regression coefficients.

    Neither method is partial with respect to a second climate variable — if you want the correlation of growth with precipitation controlling for temperature, that is what seascorr does, by season. And if your question is whether a single relationship is stable through time rather than which months matter, that is moving_corr. climate_response is the monthly, multi-variable screen; the three functions answer different questions and share machinery rather than scope.

    The month window¶

    Months use the dendroclimatological sign convention, the same end_month anchor as seascorr: positive months are the current growth year (1 = January … 12 = December) and negative months the previous year (-1 = January … -12 = December), with -13…-24 reaching into the year before that. The window is the contiguous span from start_month to end_month, so the default start_month=-6, end_month=9 is previous June through current September, and start_month=-9 would start at previous September. Every month in that span, for every climate variable, becomes one predictor.

    Significance by bootstrap¶

    Rather than analytic standard errors, significance is bootstrapped (Guiot 1991), with three choices:

    • boot="exact" (the default) holds the climate fixed and simulates the chronology 1000 times as a series with the same spectrum — hence the same autocorrelation — using the exact method of Percival & Constantine (2006), the same engine seascorr uses. Each observed coefficient is tested against that null distribution by its Weibull non-exceedance probability. This path reuses dplPy's already-validated simulation, and is the recommended default; it reports a significance flag but no confidence interval.
    • boot="std" is the classic DENDROCLIM2002 bootstrap: resample the years with replacement (chronology and climate together), refit, and take the percentile confidence interval of the coefficients. A coefficient is significant when that interval does not bracket zero (Dixon 2001).
    • boot="stationary" is the same percentile approach but with the stationary block bootstrap of Politis & Romano (1994): it resamples blocks of years, so the resampled series keeps the autocorrelation that the plain "std" bootstrap destroys. The block length is chosen automatically by the Politis & White (2004) rule and reported in result.settings["block_length"].

    boot="none" returns point estimates only. The test level is set by ci (one of 0.1, 0.05, 0.01).

    A worked example¶

    Give climate_response a year-indexed chronology and one or more monthly climate variables (each in any shape seascorr accepts — wide, tidy, or a datetime-indexed series). A dict keys several variables by name:

    import dplpy as dpl

res = dpl.climate_response(crn, {"temperature": temp, "precip": prcp},
                           start_month=-6, end_month=9,
                           method="response", boot="exact", seed=1)

res.table        # one row per (variable, month): coef, ci_lower/upper,
                 # significant, p
print(res.summary())
res.plot()

    The figure stacks one panel per climate variable, a bar per month across the window — previous-year months in lower case, current-year months in upper case, with a divider at the year boundary (the layout of Fritts 1971, Fig. 4). Significant months are filled, the rest left open, and the resampling bootstraps add a confidence-interval whisker on each bar. Temperature panels are coloured red and moisture variables blue, auto-detected from the variable names.

    Choices you make¶

    Method. "response" (the default) is the multicollinearity-aware choice and is usually what you want when several months or variables are in play; "correlation" is the simpler per-month screen, and is fine when you only have a single variable or want the most direct reading.

    Bootstrap. "exact" is the recommended default. Prefer "stationary" when you specifically want resampling-based confidence intervals that respect the chronology's persistence; "std" is the classic iid bootstrap and will tend to call more months significant precisely because it ignores that persistence.

    The window. A wider start_month…end_month span tests more months but uses more predictors, so it needs a longer record (the response function needs more years than predictors, and the exact bootstrap needs at least 31 years).

    Reproducibility. All three bootstraps are stochastic; pass a seed= for reproducible significance. The point estimates are deterministic.

    Reading the output¶

    The response and correlation functions can disagree, and the difference is informative: a month that correlates with growth on its own but drops out of the response function was probably riding on a neighbouring month that carries the real signal. Read the coefficients together with the significance, and remember that scanning a dozen-plus months is a multiple-comparisons exercise — a single month just clearing the line is weak evidence, a physically-sensible run of months is strong. The response coefficients are standardized (the effect of a one-standard-deviation change in that month's climate on the chronology), so they are comparable across months and variables.

    Validation¶

    The coefficients were checked against treeclim 2.0.8 on its own muc_spruce / muc_clim data: for both the correlation and the response function, dplPy's coefficients match treeclim's to about 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ — the full chain of z-scoring, the predictor correlation matrix, the eigendecomposition, PVP retention, the component regression, and the back-transform reproduces the reference implementation. The bootstrap confidence intervals and significance flags are stochastic and cannot be matched bit-for-bit across different random number streams; they rest on the simulation and Weibull machinery already validated for seascorr against Meko's SEASCORR. See References for the methods.

    A planned extension¶

    Component retention currently uses the PVP criterion only. Fritts' original response function selected components by percent of variance explained and used stepwise regression; offering those is a natural future option, since much of dplPy's climate work traces back to Fritts.
